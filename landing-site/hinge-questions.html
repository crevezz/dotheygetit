Hinge questions: what they are, and examples you can use tomorrow

What a hinge question is, and what a hinge point is

The hinge point is the moment where the next activity only makes sense if they understood the last thing. The hinge question is what you ask there, to the whole class, before you choose a path. A hinge has four properties.

  • It sits at a decision, usually part-way through. The next activity is different if they fail it.
  • Everyone answers, not the six with their hands up. If you only hear volunteers, you hinge the lesson on the pupils who least need you.
  • The wrong answers are diagnostic. Each distractor is a mistake you have heard, not "all of the above" or a joke option.
  • You can read it quickly. If it takes ten minutes to collect, the moment has gone.

What it is not

It is not a starter, which tells you what survived from last lesson. It is not an exit ticket, which informs tomorrow. It is not a past-paper question. You are not scoring. You are choosing a path.

Where the idea comes from

In "Designing Great Hinge Questions" (ASCD, 2015), Dylan Wiliam says to build plan B into plan A: put a hinge in the middle, get an answer from every pupil, then move on or go back.

In that article, checked on 16 November 2026, pupils take no more than two minutes to respond, and the teacher should collect and interpret the answers in 30 seconds or less. Boards, fingers, ABCD cards or a digital tool are all fine. The question matters more than the tool, and a wrong idea should not be able to hide behind a lucky right answer. Those timings are Wiliam's, not a rule from this site.

How to write one in ten minutes

Name the single idea they must have before the next task. Write the right answer, including the reason, then three wrong answers you have actually heard, each with its own reason. Two will do if you cannot think of three.

Check the right answer is right for the right reason. If two options end at the same letter but different reasons, keep them apart. "3/4 because the numerator is bigger" and "3/4 because 9/12 is more than 8/12" must not be the same choice.

Plan both branches before the lesson. Branch A: they mostly have it, here is the practice. Branch B: they have this specific wrong idea, here is the correction and a second question. If you have not planned branch B, you will just talk for longer.

Maths: fractions that look similar

Which is larger, 2/3 or 3/4?

  • A. 2/3, because thirds are bigger pieces than quarters.
  • B. 3/4, because 3 is bigger than 2.
  • C. 3/4, because 9/12 is more than 8/12.
  • D. They are equal, because both are one piece short of a whole.

C is the one you want. A ignores how many pieces. B names the larger fraction for the wrong reason. D is "one away from a whole, so the same". If the room splits between B and D, teach those two errors, then ask a fresh pair such as 3/5 against 2/3.

Science: what the current is doing

Two identical bulbs are added in series to a cell. What is true?

  • A. The current is the same through both, and they are dimmer than one bulb alone because they share the voltage.
  • B. The first bulb uses some current up, so the second is dimmer.
  • C. The current is larger, because there are two bulbs.
  • D. Current only flows if the bulbs are different.

A is the school-level model. B is "current gets used up", and it is stubborn. If a lot of boards show B, do not start the resistance calculation. Show the same number in three places, and ask again.

English: the apostrophe they think they know

Which sentence shows possession, not a missing letter?

  • A. The dog's bowl was empty.
  • B. The dog's hungry.
  • C. Its raining.
  • D. The dogs bowl was empty.

A is possession. B is "the dog is", an apostrophe in the wrong job. C is missing the apostrophe in "it's". D has none. If the class picks B, they have learnt "apostrophes make it correct". That is a five-minute reteach, not "punctuation revision".

Humanities: a source, not a vibe

A source written by the king's own clerk says the king was loved by all his people. What is the careful use of it?

  • A. Evidence of what the clerk wanted recorded, not proof that everyone loved the king.
  • B. Proof the king was popular, because it was written at the time.
  • C. Useless, because it is biased, so it should be ignored.
  • D. Proof the clerk was lying.

A is the historical move. B treats "from the time" as "true". C is the idea that a biased source has no value. If they cluster on C, ask "what can this still tell us?"

A language example, because hinges are not only for maths

Which means "she doesn't have a brother"?

  • A. Elle n'a pas de frère.
  • B. Elle n'a pas un frère.
  • C. Elle ne a pas de frère.
  • D. Elle n'a de frère pas.

A is the sentence you want: negation, and de rather than un. B keeps the indefinite article. C misses the elision. D has word-by-word order. Reteach the error that dominates, not all four.

How they should answer, and what you do next

Mini whiteboards work if boards stay down until you count. ABCD fingers, or cards in the Plickers style, work if each letter is tied to a reason. A private answer on a device is one way of checking for understanding. Ask for the letter plus five words on why, so a guess does not look like knowing who understood.

Then do what you said you would: move on, reteach a specific wrong idea, or deal with a split class. If you move on after a room full of the wrong option, the question was only a ceremony.

Questions people ask

What is a hinge question?

A hinge question is a short diagnostic question at the point in a lesson where you must decide whether to move on. Every pupil answers. The wrong options are built from mistakes you have actually seen, so the pattern of answers tells you what to do next.

What is a hinge point question?

It is the same idea under a slightly different name. The hinge point is the moment in the lesson where the next activity only makes sense if they understood the last thing. The hinge point question is the question you ask there, to the whole class, before you choose a path.

What makes a good hinge question?

One clear idea, every pupil committing quickly, and wrong options that each represent a different mistake. Pupils with the right reasoning and the wrong reasoning should not land on the same answer. You should be able to read the room fast enough to change the next part of the lesson. A question you do not act on is just a quiz item.

How is a hinge question different from an exit ticket?

A hinge sits in the middle, while you can still change today's lesson. An exit ticket sits at the end, and the decision is about the next lesson. Both should be one question that is hard to bluff. They are not interchangeable.

What are Dylan Wiliam's hinge questions?

They are the mid-lesson checks Dylan Wiliam describes in his work on formative assessment: you plan a decision point, you get an answer from every pupil, and you either move on or go back. A wrong idea should not be able to hide behind a lucky right answer. For his own account, see "Designing Great Hinge Questions" on the ASCD site.

How do pupils answer a hinge question?

Mini whiteboards, ABCD cards, fingers, or a private answer on a device all work. Everyone must answer, and you must be able to tell the answers apart. If pupils show a letter, tie each letter to a reason, or a lucky guess looks like understanding.

Try it on one class.

One lesson. One question. Two minutes. If it doesn't tell you something you didn't already know, walk away.
